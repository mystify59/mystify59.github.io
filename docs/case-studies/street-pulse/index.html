Street Pulse: feeling live music

Modular haptic floor tiles that combine vibration and light to bring audiences into street performances. From field research and tessellation studies to electronics prototyping and user testing.

An invitation to feel the performance.

Street Pulse explores how vibration and light could turn a passing encounter with a street performer into a more engaging experience. Trapezium floor modules can be arranged into different layouts, adding a physical rhythm beneath the audience’s feet. The project combines a working demonstration module with digital models of a larger installation; the overview image is a concept render.

My contribution: I carried out almost all of the user research, developed the tessellation and exterior design, prototyped the electronics, and carried out all testing and assembly. Software development was handled by other team members.

Two experiences sharing one street.

We studied performances in Covent Garden, Leicester Square, and around King’s Cross, St Pancras and Euston. Observations and interviews covered both performers and audiences: how crowds form, where people stand, what invites participation, and the work involved in setting up and moving between locations.

The research exposed two different time scales. An audience member needs a reason to stop within a brief encounter. A performer has a full day of travel, setup and repeated performances, so additional equipment must earn its place. Journey maps and an audience-density model helped connect these needs to the physical space.

Our design question became: how could we invite people to join a performance and help shape the audience’s position without the performer continually directing them? This set the direction for a low-effort, sensory invitation.

One tile, many ways to gather.

Co-design explored ways to create curiosity, wonder and excitement. The team compared spotlight systems, adjustable-height platforms and haptic floor modules, using public feedback and feasibility discussions to narrow the direction. Early sensory comparisons supported developing vibration together with lighting.

I developed the tessellation and exterior design. Sketches, small-scale modules and figures helped us explore how repeated trapezium units could form different performance spaces. Co-design with a choreographer informed the arrangements, while surface-pattern studies and perimeter lighting developed the visual identity.

Build the sensation into the structure.

I prototyped the electronics and assembled the hardware, testing how the surface, supports and actuators changed the sensation. We compared 5 mm plywood, 5 mm acrylic and 3 mm acrylic, selecting the thinner acrylic for vibration transmission. Soft rubber mats let the actuators contact the ground, so we moved to firmer individual feet.

The electronics work explored an Arduino and DRV2605L haptic driver with linear resonant actuators. Internal spaces and wiring routes had to fit around the structure. The final demonstration used one physical unit; the proposed connectors and larger tessellated platform remained part of the wider design.

Test the rhythm, then the experience.

I carried out all testing. In a 20-person preference test, participants ranked three vibration patterns while listening to the same song: a metronome, the full song signal and selected rhythmic elements. The selected-rhythm pattern ranked best, guiding us towards a more focused haptic response.

For the experience evaluation, 19 participants wore a VR headset while standing on a functional vibration module. The virtual performance let us evaluate the physical sensation in the context of the proposed larger installation.

In that prototype evaluation, 89% agreed or strongly agreed that the platform improved the viewing experience, and 94.7% felt it interacted well with the music. These were participants’ reported experiences in a simulation. The project did not establish an increase in real street audience retention, tipping or crowd-positioning effectiveness.

Connect the research to what people feel.

The project connected field research to form, electronics and evaluation. Testing changed tangible details: the surface thickness, the support feet and the choice of vibration pattern. The strongest part of the process was bringing the physical prototype into an experience that participants could respond to.

A next iteration would test connected modules during real street performances, measuring setup effort, participation and audience retention. It would also need to validate accessibility, load capacity and outdoor durability before moving towards a public installation.
