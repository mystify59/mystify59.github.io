Thermofluids: vehicle & battery cooling

Two connected studies: shaping a compact SUV through aerodynamic research, and using battery-cooling CFD to investigate pressure loss and uneven temperatures.

Part 1 / SUV aerodynamics & profile design

Researching SUV aerodynamics

I began with the trade-off at the heart of a compact SUV: useful cabin space and a raised driving position, alongside the aerodynamic challenges of a larger frontal area, exposed underbody and upright rear. My research focused on how the roofline, wheel areas and tailgate could work together without losing the vehicle’s practicality.

In the report, I compared the Volvo EX90, Tesla Model Y and Porsche Cayenne as different approaches to space, a continuous roof-to-tail profile and active aerodynamic features. This benchmarking informed the shape studies below; it did not establish a measured drag improvement for my concept.

Developing the profile

Side-profile and perspective sketches explored the body proportions before narrowing the rear shape to three alternatives. I selected a gently sloping roof with a short, near-vertical tailgate as a compromise between a smoother transition and usable luggage space.

The chosen profile led into sketches of the nose, wheel-area airflow and rear treatment. I then built a simplified, mirrored Fusion body to examine the overall form in Fluent. That initial study highlighted the rear wake and wheel areas as priorities for refinement. The proposed air dam and active features remained concept details outside the simplified model.

Part 2 / Resolving the cooling-plate trade-offs

The problem: cooling coverage versus pressure loss

The team’s second study developed a hybrid spiral–serpentine cold plate beneath a 420 × 210 × 70 mm battery block. The aim was to cover the battery effectively while controlling the resistance encountered by the coolant. We compared channel layouts, then used Ansys Discovery CFD to inspect pressure and temperature fields.

The initial dense spiral produced a reported 6.1 kPa pressure drop, with a maximum battery temperature of 45.4 °C and an average of 38.5 °C. The long route and repeated tight turns became the main hydraulic design concern. The temperature field also showed that a reasonable average could conceal uneven cooling across the battery.

Changing the geometry

The next iteration reduced the spiral density, shortened the route to 3.40 m and widened the channel. Spiral spacing increased from 4 mm to 17 mm. Rather than simply maximising channel coverage, the revised design sought to preserve heat removal with fewer turns and less flow resistance.

Two elliptical cross-sections were then compared on the revised route. Profile 1 was 8 mm wide × 6.67 mm deep; Profile 2 was 6.67 mm wide × 8 mm deep. Swapping these dimensions retained the same cross-sectional area while changing the channel orientation relative to the battery.

The report selected Profile 1, accepting its slightly higher pressure loss in return for lower reported battery temperatures. The revised runs recorded much lower pressure losses than the initial layout, while average temperatures remained close. This made the thermal–hydraulic trade-off visible rather than treating more densely packed channels as automatically better.

These values are reported simulation outputs, not physical test results. A mesh-independence study and identical boundary settings across every iteration were not documented, so the comparison indicates a design direction rather than a validated percentage improvement.

The remaining issue: uneven cooling

The revised temperature field still showed hotter regions towards the outlet side. Reducing hydraulic resistance had not removed the temperature imbalance: coolant continued to warm as it travelled through the long route.

The proposed next iteration split the large central spiral into smaller routes and moved denser channel coverage towards the outlet region. Multiple inlet and outlet arrangements were also considered to shorten local flow paths. These were recommendations for further simulation, rather than tested fixes.

Checking the model assumptions

The hand calculations provided an energy balance and pressure-loss estimate, but their temperature predictions differed sharply from the CFD. Reviewing the setup reveals differences in flow rate, heat input, battery conductivity and thermal boundaries. Those assumptions need to be aligned before the two methods can be used as a direct validation comparison.

Design learning: use pressure and temperature fields together. The simpler channel layout returned lower reported pressure losses, while the remaining thermal imbalance defined the next geometry study.
