AI-assisted CAD workflow

A reusable Codex skill for taking mechanical design briefs into native CAD, with explicit design intent, capability checks and evidence of what the model actually contains.

Make the design intent checkable.

A CAD command can report success while creating the wrong envelope, acting on an outdated body reference, or leaving geometry behind after a failed operation. I wanted an AI-assisted workflow that could explain what it intended to build and check the resulting model before continuing.

I developed the humanoid-cad-simulation skill with Codex, shaping its engineering questions, review rules and iteration criteria through mechanical examples. The scope grew from humanoid structures to parts and assemblies more broadly. This is a personal tool-development project, separate from the S00 internship work.

A brief-to-CAD loop with evidence at each step.

  1. 01

    Define the brief

    Record the function, interfaces, units, constraints and intended decision. Separate known inputs from assumptions and missing information.

  2. 02

    Plan against available tools

    Inspect the active document and live MCP capabilities. Check that each operation and its dependent steps can be executed and inspected.

  3. 03

    Build within the agreed scope

    Use native sketches, features, bodies or components at the level of detail the task needs. Preserve unrelated work and keep a recovery route.

  4. 04

    Inspect, compare and revise

    Compare the intended dimensions and state with measured geometry. Re-identify changed entities, inspect failures and report unresolved limits.

The level of checking follows the engineering decision. An envelope study needs different evidence from a load-bearing connection. Geometry-only work does not trigger a full simulation setup; a structural claim does require its own loads, materials, connections and acceptance criteria.

Separate engineering intent from CAD execution.

Technical structure of the workflow
LayerWhat it records or does
Engineering recordUnits, coordinate frames, interfaces, requirements, consequence, modelling fidelity, assumptions and evidence. Native entity IDs stay in application-specific mappings.
Codex skillGuides intake, design decisions, capability planning, human review and the choice of evidence needed before continuing.
MCP adaptersMap the requested operations to the connected application. Fusion was exercised in native trials; SOLIDWORKS and Ansys have adapter guidance, without demonstrated live solves in this case study.
Fusion bridge extensionsLocal extensions to an existing third-party MCP implementation add typed construction-plane references, targeted body/edge queries, selection evidence and material/state reporting.
Verification and recoveryCheck bounding boxes, topology, parameter response and failure residue. Report a verified volume separately from mass when the assigned material is not established.

One useful distinction was between naming a parameter and proving that it drives a feature. Another was between a readable body name and the operative reference valid for the current topology. Those distinctions changed how the workflow selected entities, handled edits and justified its final report.

Test the workflow on different kinds of geometry.

These retained Fusion views show the earlier part trials that informed the workflow. The models exercised mounting features, openings, drafted forms and cosmetic edge treatments. They are development examples; the later integration results below are a separate, bounded test pass.

Clamps, spacers and mounting feet

The sensor foot remained a conditional concept result: its basic geometry was checked, while washer and tool access, manufacturing intent and load performance still needed review.

Flanges, mounting frames and a guide

The fairlead exposed a useful capability gap. The intended offset-plane-to-sketch chain was blocked by the tool schema, so this early example used a drafted extrusion as a limited fallback. A later bridge change enabled the loft chain tested in the September integration pass.

Covers, links and cable supports

The early twin-eye link deliberately omitted structural transition fillets because individual edges could not yet be enumerated safely. That limitation drove the later selective-edge work. Rounded exterior edges on the cover and saddle are cosmetic details, not proof of structural performance.

Check the resulting model, not just the tool response.

The recorded 4 September 2026 integration pass completed 19 checks with zero failures in Autodesk Fusion. A separate static test suite recorded 39 passing tests for the bridge changes. The live pass used a temporary scratch document and checked that the original empty document was restored afterwards.

These results demonstrate CAD execution and inspection for the tested operations. They do not establish structural strength, fatigue life, manufacturability or certification. The scratch geometry was discarded after the pass; the retained example images above come from the earlier development trials.

Let failures improve the next instruction.

Across five documented iterations, the work moved from a general engineering checklist to more specific rules for coordinate checks, capability gaps, topology changes and recovery. The most useful feedback came from failures in the native application: a rejected plane reference or ambiguous cleanup could be traced to a concrete change in the skill or bridge.

The next step is to test unfamiliar briefs with independent agent runs and a consistent baseline. That would help measure reliability, conversation length and fallback behaviour. The existing record supports a working research prototype; it does not yet support a percentage time-saving claim or unattended engineering approval.
