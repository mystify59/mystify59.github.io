Aquatorium: a compact field system

A collapsible live-fish transport concept combining a support frame, individual fish bags, oxygen delivery and cooling.

Understanding the journey before designing the product.

Aquatorium is a collapsible live-fish transport concept for backcountry anglers moving between hiking and fishing. The challenge was to carry and manage the catch without making an already full equipment load harder to handle. The concept combines three individual fish bags, oxygen delivery and cooling within a folding support frame.

My individual Phase 1 work explored six concepts for supporting the transition between activities, including a floating hydro-generator. The later Aquatorium frame, enclosure prototypes, component layouts and manufacturing study are presented as the team’s development work.

Four interviews, two connected activities

Our initial research brought together interviews with two experienced hikers and two anglers. We organised quoted evidence into pain points and emerging insights, then compared equipment inventories, existing products and the stages of a trip. The participant photos below come from our project report.

Hiking / Weight, power and reliability

Carried weight limited longer trips. Power banks added bulk, while charging products could be awkward to pack or use on the move. The notes also raised concerns about improvised repairs and pointed towards modular, dependable equipment that could earn its place in the pack.

Fishing / Access in wet, messy conditions

Tools were spread across separate items and could become buried or slow to reach. Wet, messy hands made handling less comfortable and precise, while overpacking added clutter. Organisation during use mattered as much as storage: tools needed predictable access and wet-hand handling.

Turning findings into design priorities

Weight and pack space: adding fishing equipment compounds an already full hiking load. Any new function needed to justify its mass and bulk.

Access during use: buried tools, repeated unpacking and wet or dirty hands made simple tasks awkward. We prioritised predictable access and straightforward handling.

Confidence away from help: power limitations, equipment failure and improvised fixes raised reliability concerns. Modularity, protection and repair access became recurring requirements.

Mapping the whole trip

We used a journey map to compare hiking, fishing and the combined experience. The synthesis highlighted repeated changes between walking, setting up, handling equipment and carrying the catch.

  1. 1

    Fishers start off relaxed, while hikers and anglers worry about what to pack when preparing.

  2. 2

    All are excited upon initial arrival.

  3. 3

    Hikers and anglers lose some excitement over tedious terrain and weather, but recover after seeing extraordinary scenery.

  4. 4

  5. 5

Our product benchmarking compared quick-access storage, folding structures, compact powered equipment and outdoor reliability. It suggested that existing products addressed individual needs, while the transition between hiking and fishing remained a useful focus for design.

The question that focused the project

How might we support backcountry anglers during the transition between hiking and fishing?

Exploring alternatives, then choosing a direction.

My six initial concepts

My individual Phase 1 work explored six different ways to support the transition between hiking and fishing: generating power, sharing supplies, combining equipment and preparing for the next stop. These original sketches show the range of ideas before I selected one for further development.

Developing the proposed generator in CAD

Survey feedback and a follow-up interview supported developing the floating hydro-generator. Feedback about still or slow-moving water raised questions about flow conditions and possible wind generation. I developed the proposal around two modules: the turbine and a separate anchor/battery unit.

The CAD work explored assembly, retrieval and packing: a removable front guard, an accessible generator mount and a fabric tail supported by collapsible rings. I placed the battery in the anchoring module to reduce its exposure to water, with folding pegs and a rubber-lined loop proposed for different anchoring conditions. These views show the design proposal at this stage.

Within the housing, I added reinforcing ribs to hollow parts and developed M3 screw mounting for the generator holder, making the assembly details part of the concept development.

A parallel direction: managing the catch

Joe Chen’s strand explored catch storage and handling, including an anchored net and a dual-chamber bag for refreshing water. Concepts were assessed through user feedback and weighted criteria covering feasibility, problem fit, portability, durability and complexity.

Why the project changed direction

During later team prototyping, the turbine’s output proved too low to justify its size and weight. The team moved towards Aquatorium: a collapsible fish-transport concept with oxygen delivery and cooling. Portability, access and reliability remained criteria to test throughout development.

Iterating around the real components.

Four bays became three

The first frame left too little room for the electronics and made the edge sliders difficult to handle. The team moved from four bays to three to improve spacing and access.

From a basic box to a fitted enclosure

The team moved from a basic box to a fitted enclosure so that component access, openings and mounts could develop together. These three physical prototypes exposed packaging and assembly constraints that were less obvious in CAD.

Designed to be made, taken apart and maintained.

Design for manufacturing

The team developed the enclosure around a 2 mm shell, 3 mm internal supports and 1° draft. Controlled wall thickness, radiused corners and continuous geometry were proposed to support injection moulding and part release.

Design for disassembly

The frame splits into two halves joined by removable splice plates and M4 flat-head screws. The outer bag is screwed to the frame so it can be removed for cleaning or replacement. Separate handle, fish-bag and electronics subassemblies provide access to individual parts.

The electronics enclosure is a removable module, with predominantly M3 fixings and plug-in connections where maintenance is needed. The Peltier and heat sinks are thermally bonded as a cooling assembly, which is then secured into the housing with machine screws.

These details describe the team’s manufacturing and serviceability proposal. Prototype assembly informed the design; production tooling and repeated disassembly testing would be later steps.

From circuit diagram to working prototype.

The electronics were developed as two separate systems: water pumping and active cooling. Building and testing them on the bench exposed the wiring, power and packaging needs before they were integrated into the enclosure.

The cooling circuit

A 12 V battery powers the XY-T01 temperature controller, a TEC1-12703 Peltier module and two fans. The probe provides temperature feedback; the controller’s relay switches the cooling assembly. Fans move air across the cold-side and hot-side heat sinks.

  1. 01

    Set the target

    The user sets a target temperature on the XY-T01 controller.

  2. 02

    Read the probe

    The temperature probe feeds the current reading back to the controller.

  3. 03

    Switch on cooling

    The report describes activation at 1°C above the selected target; the relay switches the Peltier and fans.

  4. 04

    Return and repeat

    The cooling assembly brings the reading towards the target while the controller continues monitoring.

The pumping circuit

An ESP32-S reads load-cell feedback through an HX711 module and controls the R320 pump through a relay. Buttons and a display support setting and checking the fill operation. This circuit manages water filling separately from the temperature-control loop.

Building and packaging the prototype

The team assembled the controls and cooling hardware on the bench, then fitted the cooling unit into an insulated enclosure. These original prototype photographs show the physical wiring, heat sinks, fans and component packaging behind the concept.

Set up, use and pack away.

The concept brings three individual fish bags together with a support frame, load-cell-controlled pumping, oxygen delivery and a cooling/control enclosure. The arrangement links the physical structure to the water and electronics systems while retaining access to the main components.

  1. 01

    Load and organise

    Unfold the frame, fit the three individual fish bags and keep the controls accessible in the side compartment.

  2. 02

    Fill and monitor

    Use the load-cell-based pumping system to fill a bag to the selected target, then monitor the cooling system through its controls.

  3. 03

    Carry and pack away

    Use the folding handles for carrying; empty and dry the bags, then collapse the body for storage. The report records 22 L unfolded and 8 L folded.

The final proposal brings together separate fish storage, controlled water filling, oxygen delivery and active cooling in a collapsible format. The renders below show the intended product arrangement and appearance; the preceding prototype work records what was built and tested.
