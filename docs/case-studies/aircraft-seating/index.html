Re:Seat

Modular aircraft seating designed for repair and reuse.

Team

Keep the structure. Renew the parts.

How could an aircraft seat accommodate changing passenger needs while making worn parts easier to replace? This concept separates the supporting frame from cushioning, electronics and accessories. The aim is to keep useful components in service, configure features for different flights and make repair part of the product’s everyday operation.

My individual submission brings together the seat concept, CAD, component architecture, proposed service system and environmental evaluation. The initial research was shared by Team 12: Ikem Enebeli, Aliza Mumtaz, Joseph Wang and Leila Ali. I also participated in the industry interview. The work shown here is a design proposal supported by research and modelling.

Understand what makes a seat hard to maintain.

Our group dismantled a legacy aircraft seat and mapped its parts, materials and joining methods. Buried fixings, rivets and adhesive-bonded materials made separation difficult or destructive. Removable upholstery offered a useful contrast: the connection made routine cleaning and replacement much more accessible.

We analysed five economy-flight review videos to build illustrative passenger personas and journey maps. These highlighted legroom, hygiene, stable tray tables and reachable power. A 40-minute interview with a cabin maintenance engineer added operational considerations around modularity, maintenance cost and safety.

Together, the findings framed the challenge: simplify access to parts without sacrificing the comfort and dependable functionality passengers expect. The group’s environmental comparisons also made mass during use a priority for the subsequent concept.

Turn research into a modular architecture.

I translated the research into requirements for a base seat under 11 kg, reversible connections between different materials, replaceable accessories and two-, three- or four-seat configurations. Frequent replacement also made connector durability a central requirement. These were design targets to assess, rather than proven performance.

Early exploration divided the seat into three layers: the supporting frame, essential features and optional modules. This gave each component a purpose and a replacement route. It also exposed a tension: making features removable adds connections that must remain secure through repeated use.

I used anthropometric references to guide dimensions and explored visible interaction cues for passengers and maintenance staff. The later review identified ergonomics and maintenance cues as areas needing further development.

Resolve the frame and the parts around it.

The aluminium frame uses I-section members and cross-shafts, with cushioning supported directly above them instead of a separate seat pan. Repeated components allow two-, three- and four-seat arrangements. That flexibility introduces additional side supports, a trade-off to weigh against the benefit of shared parts.

I proposed removable expanded-polypropylene cushion cores with recycled-PET covers, a recycled carbon-fibre composite back and detachable aluminium armrests. Separating these parts was intended to support targeted replacement and material sorting. The material choices remained subject to performance and certification assessment.

Optional modules include a detachable screen or device holder and a tray that stores against the seat back. Electronics are grouped in the back to concentrate access for repair. These features explore how changing the fitted equipment could serve different journeys without redesigning the frame.

Design the return route as well as the seat.

The service proposal connects feature selection before a flight with inspection and component replacement on the ground. Staff would log damaged or stained modules, remove them and fit prepared replacements. Returned parts would be cleaned, repaired or separated according to condition, then re-enter a shared pool.

Mapping this cycle made the operational dependencies visible. Spare-module availability, demand forecasting, storage and turnaround time would determine whether the system could work. Designing a detachable component was only one part of the task; the service also needed to deliver the right replacement when required.

Use the model to guide the next prototype.

The project’s environmental comparison uses an estimated redesigned-seat mass of 10.4 kg against an 18 kg reference. Eco Audit and material-circularity analysis explored the implications of the proposed materials and component system. These are design estimates, not measurements from a built seat or an operational aircraft.

The work connected disassembly research to CAD and a proposed service cycle. Its next step would be a physical prototype to assess fit, comfort, replacement time and repeated connector use, followed by structural and certification assessment. The key lesson was that lighter components, maintainable connections and practical service logistics need to develop together.
